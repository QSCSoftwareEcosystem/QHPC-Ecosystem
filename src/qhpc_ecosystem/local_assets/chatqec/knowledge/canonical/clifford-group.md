# Clifford Group

The Clifford group is the set of unitaries that map Pauli operators to Pauli
operators under conjugation. It is the natural symmetry group of stabilizer
codes: Clifford gates preserve the stabilizer structure, are efficiently
classically simulable, and yet are *not* universal — the gap they leave is
exactly what [[magic-state-distillation]] fills.

## Definition

The $n$-qubit Clifford group is the normalizer of the [[pauli-group]] in the
unitary group:

$$
\mathcal{C}_n = \{\, U : U P U^{\dagger} \in \mathcal{P}_n \ \text{for all}\ P \in \mathcal{P}_n \,\}
$$

up to global phase. A Clifford unitary is fully specified by how it transforms a
generating set of Paulis (e.g. $X_i, Z_i$), so it has an efficient
$2n \times 2n$ binary symplectic description — the basis of the tableau
simulation in Stim.

## Generators

The whole group is generated by just three gates:

- **Hadamard** $H$: swaps $X \leftrightarrow Z$.
- **Phase** $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)$: maps $X \to Y$, fixes $Z$.
- **CNOT**: propagates $X$ and $Z$ between control and target.

Any Clifford circuit is a product of these.

## The Gottesman-Knill theorem

A circuit built only of Clifford gates, Pauli-basis state preparation, and
measurement can be **simulated efficiently on a classical computer** — its state
is tracked by updating the stabilizer tableau in $O(n^2)$ per gate. This is why
Stim can simulate million-qubit stabilizer circuits, and why syndrome extraction
(all Clifford) is classically tractable to sample.

## Why Clifford alone is not universal

Clifford gates generate only a discrete, finite group; they cannot approximate
an arbitrary single-qubit rotation. Universal quantum computation needs at least
one **non-Clifford** gate — canonically the $T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})$
gate. On most codes $T$ cannot be applied transversally (Eastin-Knill; see
[[transversal-gates]]), so it is supplied via
[[magic-state-distillation]] and gate teleportation. This Clifford + $T$
decomposition is why non-Clifford resources (T-count) dominate the cost of
fault-tolerant algorithms.
